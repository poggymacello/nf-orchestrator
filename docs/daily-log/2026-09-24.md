# 2026-09-24 — Day 30

**Focus:** The limit I accepted yesterday: `nf_cluster_probe_seconds` can't tell a slow cluster from
a slow orchestrator host. I wanted to find out what that limit costs before deciding whether to
keep accepting it.

**Studied:** What client-go logs at `-v=6`, how CPU affinity is inherited by child processes on
Windows, and what a killed subprocess leaves behind in its captured stderr.

**Did:** Hypotheses first. H1: starving only the orchestrator's CPU pushes the probe past 0.5s and
pages `ClusterThrottlingOrchestrator` for a healthy cluster. H2: client-go's own round-trip number
stays low meanwhile, and it's available from the same process. H3: on a starved enough host the
probe never even sends its request, and day 29 counts that as a slow cluster. H4: releases go
unreported for `deadline`, which for once is the right runbook. H5: drill 10 must still read as
busy afterwards. H6: the lab's confound — kind runs on this same machine — has to be checked, not
assumed away.

A new throwaway cluster with ten releases. The orchestrator, then eight busy loops, all pinned to
core 0; kubectl and helm inherit the pin. H6 first: from an unpinned shell, `/readyz` answered in
5–8ms with the burners running. The cluster was fine.

H1 held completely. Busy on every scrape, and `ClusterThrottlingOrchestrator` firing at 03:17:48Z.
H2 held: pinned and unbounded, kubectl took ~1.1s of wall time for a 31–80ms round trip. H3 was
worse than I wrote it: 8 of 8 probes killed at 0.75s, and 7 had not logged a single line. The
cluster was never asked.

The fix reads the round trip out of kubectl's own log. The cluster is judged on that; the rest is
reported as the host's, in a new series with a new alert. The scrape's probe gets 3s, because it
runs beside the work and doesn't need a failed call's 0.75s.

**Got stuck on:** the fix working and the signal staying wrong. Round trip 40ms, host 1.2s, and
`nf_cluster_busy` still 1 on four scrapes in six. Every per-release reason was 0, which left one
suspect — the batched pod listing. It overran its 3s bound on the starved host, the probe answered,
and day 28's rule said "the server answers its probe, so it must be refusing us". The probe had
answered in 40ms. So a timed-out call now has a third outcome, `HostOverloaded`, checked before the
two that blame the cluster.

That broke five of my own tests from days 28 and 29, and they turned out to be the thing that was
wrong. Each faked a stalled server as a timeout with nothing logged. I measured the real one: drill
7's stall server gets 11 log lines out of kubectl before the kill, every time. An empty log is what a
starved host looks like. The fakes had been describing drill 11 all along, and nothing could tell
until the code learned the difference.

Verified: busy 0 on 8 of 8 starved scrapes, `OrchestratorHostOverloaded` firing,
`ClusterThrottlingOrchestrator` and `ClusterUnreachable` quiet. Drill 7's stall server still
unreachable. Drill 10's queueing, rebuilt on this cluster, still busy on every scrape, with a round
trip of 1.0–2.2s against a host share of 0.1s — which also answers what drill 10 left open about its
own load generators.

**Learned:** Every duration measured through a child process is two durations added together, and
I had been reading the sum as the cluster's. The number that separates them was already being
printed by kubectl; I was throwing it away with the rest of stderr. And a test fake is a claim about
the world, like any other: mine was never measured, so it quietly described the wrong incident for
two days.
